Adic Space I

Valuations, Huber pairs, and rational subsets

From valuation-theoretic points to affinoid adic spaces and their structure sheaves.

1 Valuations

1.1 Curves and valuation rings

Let \(k\) be a field. The basic algebraic-geometric model is the correspondence between curves over \(k\) and function fields of one variable over \(k\).

Theorem 1 (The curve–function field correspondence) The functor \(C\mapsto K(C)\) gives a contravariant correspondence between nonsingular proper integral curves over \(k\) and finitely generated field extensions \(K/k\) of transcendence degree one.

After choosing a transcendental element \(t\in K\), such a field \(K\) is a finite extension of \(k(t)\).

Let \(C\) be such a curve and put \(K=K(C)\). A point \(x\in C\) determines the local ring \(\mathcal O_{C,x}\subseteq K\).

For a closed point \(x\), the local ring \(\mathcal O_{C,x}\) is a discrete valuation ring, and \(\mathcal O_{C,x}=\{f\in K\mid \operatorname{ord}_x(f)\geq0\}\).

For every open subset \(U\subseteq C\), one has \(\mathcal O_C(U)=\bigcap_{x\in U}\mathcal O_{C,x}\subseteq K\). Thus regularity on \(U\) is detected pointwise by valuation rings.

Definition 1 (Valuation ring) Let \(K\) be a field. A subring \(R\subseteq K\) is a valuation ring if, for every \(a\in K^\times\), either \(a\in R\) or \(a^{-1}\in R\).

Its group of units is \(R^\times\), and the quotient \(\Gamma_R=K^\times/R^\times\) is naturally a totally ordered abelian group.

The associated valuation may be written additively as \(v:K^\times\to\Gamma_R\), or multiplicatively as \(|\cdot|:K\to\Gamma_R\cup\{0\}\).

Definition 2 (Rank of a valuation) The rank of a totally ordered abelian group \(\Gamma\) is the length of the chain of its proper convex subgroups. The rank of a valuation is the rank of its value group.

Example 1  

  1. If \(\Gamma=\mathbb Z\), then the corresponding valuation ring is a discrete valuation ring.

  2. Every nonzero ordered subgroup \(\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb R\) is archimedean and has rank one. Examples include \(\mathbb Z\), \(\mathbb Q\), and \(\mathbb R\).

  3. Give \(\mathbb R\oplus\mathbb R\varepsilon\) the lexicographic order: \((a,b)<(a',b')\) if \(a<a'\), or if \(a=a'\) and \(b<b'\). The subgroup \(\mathbb R\varepsilon\) is convex, so this group has rank two.

  4. Similarly, \(\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z\varepsilon\) with the lexicographic order is a discrete ordered group of rank two.

In multiplicative notation, a lexicographically ordered rank-two group can be written using symbols \(r\gamma^n\), where the ordinary real scale \(r\) is compared first and the power of \(\gamma\) breaks ties.

1.2 Classical points in rigid geometry

Let \(K\) be a complete nonarchimedean field. Important examples are finite extensions of \(\mathbb Q_p\) and the completed algebraic closure \(\mathbb C_p\).

Consider the rigid closed unit disc \(\mathbb D=\operatorname{Sp}(K\langle T\rangle)\).

Example 2 A classical point \(x\) is represented by a maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m_x\subseteq K\langle T\rangle\). Its residue field \(K(x)=K\langle T\rangle/\mathfrak m_x\) is a finite extension of \(K\).

The absolute value of \(K\) induces uniquely \(|f|_x=|\overline f|_{K(x)}\), where \(\overline f\) denotes the image of \(f\) in \(K(x)\).

In this way, a classical point produces a multiplicative seminorm \(|\cdot|_x:K\langle T\rangle\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}\).

1.3 Two enlargements of the set of points

1.3.1 Berkovich points

Definition 3 (Berkovich spectrum) Let \(A\) be a Banach \(K\)-algebra. Its Berkovich spectrum \(\mathcal M(A)\) consists of bounded multiplicative seminorms \(|\cdot|_x:A\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}\) whose restriction to \(K\) is the given absolute value.

Thus Berkovich geometry enlarges the classical points by allowing arbitrary real-valued continuous seminorms.

1.3.2 Adic points

Adic geometry makes a different enlargement: the points are (multiplicative) continuous valuations of arbitrary rank.

Let \(\varphi:B\to A\) be a continuous morphism of affinoid algebras. A valuation \(|\cdot|_x:A\to\Gamma_x\cup\{0\}\) pulls back to \(|\cdot|_y=|\cdot|_x\circ\varphi:B\to\Gamma_x\cup\{0\}\).

The support of the pulled-back valuation is \(\operatorname{supp}(y)=\varphi^{-1}(\operatorname{supp}(x))\). Notice that the support prime alone does not determine an adic point: the valuation itself is part of the data.

1.4 New points on the closed disc

1.4.1 A higher-rank boundary point

Let \(\pi\in K\) be a pseudo-uniformizer. At the level of classical rigid points, write

\[ \mathbb D^-=\bigcup_{n\geq1}\mathbb D(0,|\pi|^{1/n}), \qquad \partial\mathbb D=\operatorname{Sp}K\langle T,T^{-1}\rangle. \]

Then \(\mathbb D=\mathbb D^-\sqcup\partial\mathbb D\) as a set of classical points, but this is not an admissible rigid covering.

To see the obstruction pointwise, introduce the ordered group \(\Gamma=\mathbb R_{>0}\oplus\gamma^{\mathbb Z}\) with lexicographic order

\[ r\gamma^n<r'\gamma^{n'} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r<r' \ \text{or}\ (r=r'\text{ and }n<n'). \]

In particular, \(\gamma>1\), while \(\gamma\) is infinitesimally close to \(1\) relative to the ordinary real scale.

Example 3 (The point \(x^-\)) For \(f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nT^n\in K\langle T\rangle\), define

\[ |f|_{x^-}=\max_n\bigl(|a_n|\gamma^{-n}\bigr). \]

Then \(|T|_{x^-}=\gamma^{-1}<1\), but \(|T|_{x^-}=\gamma^{-1}>|\pi|^{1/n}\) for every \(n\geq1\). Hence \(x^-\) does not belong to any disc \(\mathbb D(0,|\pi|^{1/n})\).

Moreover, \(|T^{-1}|_{x^-}=\gamma>1\), so \(x^-\) does not belong to \(\partial\mathbb D\) either.

After adjoining \(x^-\), the failure of \(\mathbb D^-\cup\partial\mathbb D\) to cover \(\mathbb D\) is visible on the underlying set of points.

1.4.2 Rank-one Gauss points

Let \(\mathbb D(a,r)\subseteq\mathbb D\) be a closed ball. If \(f(T)=\sum_{n\geq0}b_n(T-a)^n\), define the associated Gauss point \(x_{a,r}\) by \(|f|_{x_{a,r}}=\max_n|b_n|r^n\).

When the points of the ball are sufficiently rich, this is also \(|f|_{x_{a,r}}=\sup_{z\in\mathbb D(a,r)}|f(z)|\).

Example 4 The inclusion \(\mathbb D(0,|\pi|)\hookrightarrow\mathbb D\) corresponds contravariantly to

\[ K\langle T\rangle \longrightarrow K\langle T,T'\rangle/(T'-\pi^{-1}T) \simeq K\langle T,T'\rangle/(T-\pi T'). \]

The Gauss point of this smaller disc records the supremum norm on the entire disc, not evaluation at a single classical point.

The point \(x_{0,1}\) is the usual Tate norm. One can also refine a radius infinitesimally and obtain points customarily denoted \(x_{r^-}\) and \(x_{r^+}\); for example, \(x_{1^+}\) lies outside the closed unit disc.

Remark 1 (Why Gauss points are useful). A Gauss point behaves like the generic point of a closed ball. It simultaneously records all inequalities valid on that ball, makes nested families of discs visible topologically, and turns many admissibility questions into ordinary questions about whether every point is covered.

2 Huber pairs and adic spectra

2.1 Huber rings

Definition 4 (Huber ring) A Huber ring is a topological ring \(A\) admitting an open subring \(A_0\subseteq A\) and a finitely generated ideal \(I\subseteq A_0\) such that the \(I\)-adic topology on \(A_0\) induces the topology of \(A\).

The ring \(A_0\) is called a ring of definition, and \(I\) is called an ideal of definition.

An element \(a\in A\) is power-bounded if the set \(\{a^n:n\geq0\}\) is bounded. The subring of power-bounded elements is denoted \(A^\circ\).

The ideal of topologically nilpotent elements is denoted \(A^{\circ\circ}\).

Definition 5 (Huber pair) A ring of integral elements is an open subring \(A^+\subseteq A^\circ\) that is integrally closed in \(A\).

A Huber pair is a pair \((A,A^+)\) consisting of a Huber ring and a ring of integral elements.

Definition 6 (Adic spectrum) For a Huber pair \((A,A^+)\), the adic spectrum \(\operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+)\) is the set of equivalence classes of continuous valuations \(|\cdot|_x:A\to\Gamma_x\cup\{0\}\) such that \(|a(x)|\leq1\) for every \(a\in A^+\).

Two valuations are equivalent if they induce the same ordering on the elements of \(A\).

Definition 7 (Tate ring) A Huber ring \(A\) is a Tate ring if it contains a topologically nilpotent unit. Such a unit is called a pseudo-uniformizer.

2.2 Basic examples

Example 5 Let \(A=K\langle T_1,\ldots,T_n\rangle\) be a Tate ring, and a natural Huber pair is

\[ (A,A^+)= \bigl( K\langle T_1,\ldots,T_n\rangle, \mathcal O_K\langle T_1,\ldots,T_n\rangle \bigr). \]

We can see that \(\operatorname{Sp}(A)\subset \operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+)\) as sets.

In general, let \(A\) be an affinoid algebra equipped with the topology induced by one reduced norm \(|\cdot|\), then \(A^\circ\) is the integral closure in \(A\) of the subring consisting of the elements \(a\in A\) such that \(|a|\leq 1\). Thus, we can take \(A^+=A^\circ\).

Example 6 Let \(R\) be complete and separated for the \(I\)-adic topology, where \(I\) is finitely generated. Then \(R\) is a Huber ring with ring of definition \(R\), and \((R,R)\) is a Huber pair.

Examples include \(\mathbb Z_p\) and \(\mathbb Z_p\llbracket X_1,\ldots,X_n\rrbracket\) with their natural adic topologies. These rings are not Tate rings because they contain no topologically nilpotent unit.

2.3 Raynaud’s formal models

Let \(A_0=\mathcal O_K\langle T_1,\ldots,T_n\rangle/\mathfrak a\) be a topologically finite-type \(\mathcal O_K\)-algebra, complete for the \(\pi\)-adic topology. Then

\[ A_0 \simeq \varprojlim_m A_0/\pi^mA_0 \simeq \varprojlim_m \mathcal O_K[T_1,\ldots,T_n]/(\pi^m,\mathfrak a_m), \]

where \(\mathfrak a_m\) denotes the image of \(\mathfrak a\) modulo \(\pi^m\). After inverting \(\pi\), we obtain the affinoid \(K\)-algebra \(A=A_0[1/\pi]\simeq K\langle T_1,\ldots,T_n\rangle/\mathfrak a_K\). If \(A^+\) denotes the integral closure of the image of \(A_0\) in \(A\), the analytic generic fiber of \(\mathfrak X=\operatorname{Spf}(A_0)\) is the affinoid adic space \(X_\eta=\operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+)\). This construction is the local algebraic mechanism underlying the Raynaud formal–rigid correspondence.

Remark 2. The Tate algebra \(K\langle T_1,\ldots,T_n\rangle\) is noetherian. If the valuation of \(K\) is not discrete, however, the integral model \(\mathcal O_K\langle T_1,\ldots,T_n\rangle\) need not be noetherian.

3 Rational subsets

3.1 Definition and topology

Definition 8 (Rational subset) Let \(X=\operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+)\). Choose \(s,t_1,\ldots,t_n\in A\) such that the ideal \((s,t_1,\ldots,t_n)\) is open. The associated rational subset is

\[ U\left(\frac{t_1,\ldots,t_n}{s}\right) = \left\{ x\in X: |t_i(x)|\leq|s(x)|\neq0 \text{ for }1\leq i\leq n \right\}. \]

Rational subsets form a basis for the topology of \(\operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+)\) and are stable under finite intersection.

3.2 Rational localization

3.2.1 The rigid-geometric model

The universal property of a rational localization is modeled on the corresponding argument for an affinoid subdomain in rigid geometry.

Let \(X=\operatorname{Sp}(A)\) and consider the rational affinoid subdomain

\[ U= U\left(\frac{t_1,\ldots,t_n}{s}\right) = \operatorname{Sp} A\left\langle\frac{t_1,\ldots,t_n}{s}\right\rangle, \]

where

\[ A\left\langle\frac{t_1,\ldots,t_n}{s}\right\rangle = \frac{A\langle T_1,\ldots,T_n\rangle} {(sT_1-t_1,\ldots,sT_n-t_n)}. \]

Suppose that \(\varphi:Y=\operatorname{Sp}(B)\to X\) is a morphism whose image is contained in \(U\). For every maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m\subseteq B\), the associated rigid point \(y\in Y\) satisfies

\[ |\varphi^\#(t_i)(y)| \leq |\varphi^\#(s)(y)| \neq0. \]

If \(\varphi^\#(s)\) were not invertible, it would belong to some maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m\subseteq B\). The corresponding rigid point would then satisfy \(|\varphi^\#(s)(y)|=0\), contradicting the assumption that \(\varphi(y)\in U\). Hence \(\varphi^\#(s)\in B^\times\).

We may therefore define \(b_i=\varphi^\#(t_i)/\varphi^\#(s)\). The inequalities above imply that \(b_i\) is power-bounded. By the universal property of the Tate algebra, there is a unique continuous homomorphism

\[ A\langle T_1,\ldots,T_n\rangle \longrightarrow B, \qquad T_i\longmapsto \frac{\varphi^\#(t_i)}{\varphi^\#(s)}. \]

Since \(sT_i-t_i\) maps to zero, this homomorphism factors uniquely through \(A\langle t_1/s,\ldots,t_n/s\rangle\). This is the classical proof of the universal property of a rational affinoid subdomain.

3.2.2 The adic construction

Now let \(X=\operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+)\) and let

\[ U= U\left(\frac{t_1,\ldots,t_n}{s}\right) = \left\{ x\in X: |t_i(x)|\leq|s(x)|\neq0 \text{ for every }i \right\}. \]

Fix a ring of definition \(A_0\subseteq A\) and an ideal of definition \(I\subseteq A_0\). Inside \(A[1/s]\), put

\[ A_{0,U} = A_0\left[\frac{t_1}{s},\ldots,\frac{t_n}{s}\right]. \]

Equip \(A[1/s]\) with the adic topology for which \(A_{0,U}\) is a ring of definition and \(IA_{0,U}\) is an ideal of definition. Its separated completion is denoted

\[ A_U = A\left\langle\frac{t_1,\ldots,t_n}{s}\right\rangle. \]

Before completion, define \(A(T/s)^+\) to be the integral closure of \(A^+[t_1/s,\ldots,t_n/s]\) in \(A[1/s]\). The completed positive part \(A_U^+\) is obtained by taking the closure of its image in \(A_U\) and then its integral closure in \(A_U\).

Example 7 For a Tate affinoid algebra, the underlying rational localization has the familiar presentation

\[ A\left\langle\frac{t_1,\ldots,t_n}{s}\right\rangle = \frac{A\langle T_1,\ldots,T_n\rangle} {(sT_1-t_1,\ldots,sT_n-t_n)}. \]

The point of first considering the algebraic construction is that completion does not change the adic spectrum:

\[ \operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+) \simeq \operatorname{Spa}(\widehat A,\widehat{A}^+). \]

Thus the desired factorization can first be constructed algebraically and only then extended by completion.

3.2.3 Attempting the rigid proof

Let \((B,B^+)\) be a separated complete Huber pair, and let \(\varphi:(A,A^+)\to(B,B^+)\) be a continuous morphism such that the image of

\[ \operatorname{Spa}(\varphi): \operatorname{Spa}(B,B^+) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+) \]

is contained in \(U\). Then, for every \(y\in\operatorname{Spa}(B,B^+)\),

\[ |\varphi^\#(t_i)(y)| \leq |\varphi^\#(s)(y)| \neq0. \]

To imitate the rigid proof, we first need to show that \(\varphi^\#(s)\) is invertible. The obvious attempt is to argue using maximal ideals of \(B\), exactly as in the affinoid case.

Remark 3 (Where the rigid argument breaks). A maximal ideal \(\mathfrak m\subseteq B\) defines an algebraic valuation

\[ B\longrightarrow B/\mathfrak m \longrightarrow\{0,1\}. \]

However, this valuation is continuous only when \(\mathfrak m\) is open. Consequently, one does not have an inclusion \(\operatorname{Max}(B)\subseteq\operatorname{Spa}(B,B^+)\) in general.

Therefore, the classical argument

\[ |f(y)|\neq0\text{ for every }y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f\notin\mathfrak m\text{ for every maximal ideal }\mathfrak m \]

cannot simply be repeated in the adic setting.

The failure of the maximal-ideal argument does not mean that pointwise nonvanishing fails to detect units. It means that a different proof, using the topology and completeness of \(B\), is required.

3.2.4 Repairing the argument

Lemma 1 (Nonvanishing detects units) Let \((B,B^+)\) be a separated complete Huber pair. For \(f\in B\), the following conditions are equivalent:

  1. \(f\) is invertible in \(B\);
  2. \(|f(y)|\neq0\) for every \(y\in\operatorname{Spa}(B,B^+)\).

Proof. If \(f\) is invertible, then it cannot vanish at any valuation, so only the converse requires proof.

Assume that \(f\) is not invertible. We first claim that the principal ideal \(fB\) is not dense in \(B\). Indeed, if \(fB\) were dense, then for a ring of definition \(B_0\) and an ideal of definition \(J\subseteq B_0\), one could choose \(g\in B\) such that \(1-fg\in J\). Since \(J\) is topologically nilpotent and \(B\) is complete, the geometric series

\[ (fg)^{-1} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(1-fg)^n \]

converges in \(B\). Thus \(fg\) would be invertible, forcing \(f\) itself to be invertible, a contradiction.

It follows that \(\overline{fB}\) is a proper closed ideal. Hence

\[ C=B/\overline{fB} \]

is a nonzero separated complete Huber ring. Let \(C^+\) be a ring of integral elements obtained from the image of \(B^+\) in \(C\). Since a nonzero complete Huber pair has a nonempty adic spectrum, there exists \(z\in\operatorname{Spa}(C,C^+)\).

Pulling \(z\) back along \(B\to C\) gives a point \(y\in\operatorname{Spa}(B,B^+)\) at which \(f\) vanishes, because the image of \(f\) in \(C\) is zero. This contradicts the assumption that \(|f(y)|\neq0\) for every \(y\). Therefore \(f\) is invertible. \(\square\)

Example 8 (Why completeness is essential) The completeness assumption cannot be omitted. Give \(K[T]\) the Huber topology with ring of definition \(\mathcal O_K[T]\) and ideal of definition \(\pi\mathcal O_K[T]\).

The element \(1+\pi T\) is not invertible in \(K[T]\), but every \(x\in\operatorname{Spa}(K[T],\mathcal O_K[T])\) satisfies \(|\pi T(x)|<1\), and hence \(|1+\pi T|_x=1\).

Its inverse \(\sum_{n\geq0}(-\pi T)^n\) appears only after passing to the completion \(K\langle T\rangle\).

3.2.5 Completing the factorization

Apply the lemma to \(f=\varphi^\#(s)\). Since \(|\varphi^\#(s)(y)|\neq0\) for every \(y\in\operatorname{Spa}(B,B^+)\), we obtain \(\varphi^\#(s)\in B^\times\).

Define \(b_i=\varphi^\#(t_i)/\varphi^\#(s)\). The assumption that the image lies in \(U\) gives \(|b_i(y)|\leq1\) for every \(y\in\operatorname{Spa}(B,B^+)\).

For a complete Huber pair, the positive subring can be recovered from its adic points:

\[ B^+ = \left\{ b\in B: |b(y)|\leq1 \text{ for every }y\in\operatorname{Spa}(B,B^+) \right\}. \]

Consequently, \(b_i\in B^+\). At the algebraic level, we now have

\[ A[T_1,\ldots,T_n]\longrightarrow B, \qquad T_i\longmapsto b_i, \]

and this map annihilates every relation \(sT_i-t_i\). Equivalently, the original map \(A\to B\) extends uniquely to

\[ A[1/s]\longrightarrow B, \qquad \frac{t_i}{s}\longmapsto b_i. \]

Because only finitely many elements \(b_i\) occur and each \(b_i\) is power-bounded, we may choose a ring of definition \(B_0\subseteq B\) such that \(\varphi^\#(A_0)\subseteq B_0\) and \(b_i\in B_0\) for every \(i\). We then obtain a continuous map of rings of definition

\[ A_0\left[\frac{t_1}{s},\ldots,\frac{t_n}{s}\right] \longrightarrow B_0. \]

Since \(B\) is complete, this map extends uniquely across the separated completion to a continuous homomorphism \(A_U\to B\). Moreover, the map sends \(A^+[t_1/s,\ldots,t_n/s]\) into \(B^+\); because \(B^+\) is integrally closed, it also sends \(A_U^+\) into \(B^+\). We therefore obtain a morphism of Huber pairs

\[ (A_U,A_U^+)\longrightarrow(B,B^+). \]

Passing to adic spectra gives the desired factorization

\[ \operatorname{Spa}(B,B^+) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Spa}(A_U,A_U^+) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+). \]

The first arrow is unique because its effect on the dense subring \(A[1/s]\) is forced by the assignments \(t_i/s\mapsto b_i\).

Finally, every \(x\in U\) extends uniquely to a valuation on \(A[1/s]\) satisfying \(|t_i/s|_x\leq1\), and then uniquely to its completion \(A_U\). Conversely, every point of \(\operatorname{Spa}(A_U,A_U^+)\) restricts to a point of \(U\). Thus the rational localization has precisely the required underlying set of points.

Proposition 1 (Universal property of a rational localization) The natural morphism

\[ \operatorname{Spa}(A_U,A_U^+) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+) \]

identifies \(\operatorname{Spa}(A_U,A_U^+)\) with the rational subset \(U\).

For every separated complete Huber pair \((B,B^+)\) and every continuous morphism \(\varphi:(A,A^+)\to(B,B^+)\) whose induced map on adic spectra has image contained in \(U\), there is a unique factorization

\[ (A,A^+) \longrightarrow (A_U,A_U^+) \longrightarrow (B,B^+). \]

4 The structure presheaf

4.1 Definition on rational subsets

Let \(X=\operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+)\). For a rational subset \(U=U(t_1,\ldots,t_n/s)\), define \(\mathcal O_X(U)=A\langle t_1/s,\ldots,t_n/s\rangle\). The corresponding ring of integral elements is denoted \(\mathcal O_X^+(U)\).

For an arbitrary open subset \(W\subseteq X\), define the presheaf by

\[ \mathcal O_X(W) = \varprojlim_{\substack{U\subseteq W\\ U\text{ rational}}} \mathcal O_X(U). \]

4.2 Sheafiness

4.2.1 The presheaf on the rational site

Let \(X=\operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+)\). At this stage, the structure presheaf is first defined only on rational subsets of \(X\).

Denote by \(\operatorname{Rat}(X)\) the category whose objects are rational subsets of \(X\) and whose morphisms are inclusions. A family \(\{U_i\to U\}\) is declared to be a covering if \(U=\bigcup_iU_i\). In this way, \(\operatorname{Rat}(X)\) becomes a Grothendieck site.

Rational subsets form a basis for the ordinary topology of \(X\), are quasi-compact, and are stable under finite intersections. Consequently, when checking the sheaf condition on \(\operatorname{Rat}(X)\), it is enough to consider finite rational coverings.

Rational localization gives a presheaf \(\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}\) on this site: if

\[ U= U\left(\frac{t_1,\ldots,t_n}{s}\right), \]

then

\[ \mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U) = A\left\langle\frac{t_1,\ldots,t_n}{s}\right\rangle. \]

Whenever \(V\subseteq U\) are rational subsets, the universal property of rational localization produces the restriction map \(\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U)\to\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(V)\).

4.2.2 Tate acyclicity and the sheaf condition

The first question is whether \(\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}\) is a sheaf on the rational site. In the rigid-analytic situation, this is precisely where Tate acyclicity enters.

Theorem 2 (Tate acyclicity on rational coverings) Suppose that \(A\) is a strongly noetherian Tate ring. Let \(U\subseteq X\) be rational and let \(U=U_1\cup\cdots\cup U_r\) be a finite rational covering. Then the augmented Čech complex

\[ 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U) \longrightarrow \prod_i\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U_i) \longrightarrow \prod_{i,j}\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U_i\cap U_j) \longrightarrow \prod_{i,j,k}\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U_i\cap U_j\cap U_k) \longrightarrow\cdots \]

is exact.

In particular, its degree-zero part is the equalizer sequence

\[ 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U) \longrightarrow \prod_i\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U_i) \rightrightarrows \prod_{i,j}\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U_i\cap U_j). \]

This is exactly the sheaf condition on the rational site: sections that agree on pairwise intersections glue uniquely. Thus Tate acyclicity implies that \(\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}\) is a sheaf on \(\operatorname{Rat}(X)\).

4.2.3 Extension from the rational basis

Once \(\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}\) is known to be a sheaf on the rational basis, it extends uniquely to a sheaf on the entire topological space \(X\).

Let \(W\subseteq X\) be an arbitrary open subset and write

\[ \operatorname{Rat}(W) = \left\{ U\subseteq W: U\text{ is rational in }X \right\}. \]

The rational subsets contained in \(W\) form a covering of \(W\). Since the intersection of two rational subsets is again rational, the sheaf condition gives the equalizer sequence

\[ 0 \longrightarrow \mathcal O_X(W) \longrightarrow \prod_{U\in\operatorname{Rat}(W)} \mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U) \rightrightarrows \prod_{U,V\in\operatorname{Rat}(W)} \mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U\cap V). \]

An element of this equalizer is precisely a family of sections \(f_U\in\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U)\), one for every rational \(U\subseteq W\), such that \(f_U|_{U\cap V}=f_V|_{U\cap V}\) for all \(U,V\). Equivalently,

\[ \mathcal O_X(W) \simeq \varprojlim_{\substack{U\subseteq W\\U\text{ rational}}} \mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U). \]

Thus the inverse limit is not an unrelated definition: it is forced by the unique extension of a sheaf from the rational basis to the whole topology.

If \(W\) itself is rational, then \(W\) is the largest object in \(\operatorname{Rat}(W)\), so the formula recovers the original rational localization:

\[ \mathcal O_X(W) \simeq \mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(W). \]

When the rings of sections are regarded as topological rings, \(\mathcal O_X(W)\) is equipped with the projective-limit topology.

4.2.4 The general definition

The preceding argument suggests what to do for an arbitrary Huber pair, even when Tate acyclicity has not been established.

Starting from the rational-localization presheaf \(\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}\), define, for every open subset \(W\subseteq X\),

\[ \mathcal O_X(W) := \varprojlim_{\substack{U\subseteq W\\U\text{ rational}}} \mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}(U). \]

This construction always produces a presheaf on the ordinary topology of \(X\), and it agrees with the original rational-localization presheaf on every rational subset. It need not, however, satisfy the sheaf condition.

Definition 9 (Sheafy Huber pair) A Huber pair \((A,A^+)\) is sheafy if the presheaf \(\mathcal O_X\) defined above is a sheaf on \(X=\operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+)\).

Equivalently, \((A,A^+)\) is sheafy if the rational-localization presheaf \(\mathcal O_X^{\mathrm{rat}}\) satisfies the sheaf condition for rational coverings.

The same construction applies to the integral structure presheaf. Once \(\mathcal O_X\) is a sheaf, one may describe it pointwise by

\[ \mathcal O_X^+(W) = \left\{ f\in\mathcal O_X(W): |f(x)|\leq1 \text{ for every }x\in W \right\}. \]

4.2.5 Standard sheafy classes

Theorem 3 (Standard sheafiness criteria) Let \((A,A^+)\) be a complete Huber pair. Its structure presheaf is a sheaf in each of the following situations:

  1. \(A\) is discrete;
  2. \(A\) has a noetherian ring of definition;
  3. \(A\) is a strongly noetherian Tate ring.

Definition 10 (Strongly noetherian) A Tate ring \(A\) is strongly noetherian if \(A\langle T_1,\ldots,T_n\rangle\) is noetherian for every \(n\geq0\), where

\[ A\langle T_1,\ldots,T_n\rangle = \left\{ \sum_{\nu\in\mathbb N^n}a_\nu T^\nu: a_\nu\in A,\ a_\nu\longrightarrow0 \right\}. \]

Example 9  

  1. A discrete ring gives the adic-space incarnation of an affine scheme.

  2. A complete adic ring with a noetherian ring of definition gives the local model arising from a locally noetherian formal scheme.

  3. Every classical affinoid \(K\)-algebra is strongly noetherian. Hence its associated Huber pair \((A,A^\circ)\) is sheafy. In this case, the sheaf condition is exactly the degree-zero consequence of the usual Tate acyclicity theorem from rigid geometry.

  4. More generally, stably uniform Tate Huber pairs are sheafy. In particular, affinoid perfectoid algebras provide an important class of sheafy Huber pairs beyond the noetherian setting.

4.2.6 From the rigid \(G\)-topology to a genuine topology

The sheafiness of classical affinoid algebras explains only one part of the passage from rigid spaces to adic spaces. Tate acyclicity proves that analytic functions satisfy descent, but it does not by itself produce an ordinary topological space on which this descent takes place.

In classical rigid geometry, the points of \(\operatorname{Sp}(A)\) are essentially the maximal ideals of \(A\). Rational subdomains and admissible coverings cannot be recovered from the naive topology on these classical points, so they must instead be organized into a Grothendieck topology, the rigid \(G\)-topology.

The associated adic spectrum

\[ X^{\mathrm{ad}} = \operatorname{Spa}(A,A^\circ) \]

contains the classical rigid points but also the additional valuation-theoretic points. Under this enlargement, rigid rational subdomains become genuine rational open subsets:

\[ \left\{ \begin{array}{c} \text{rational subdomains}\\ \text{of }\operatorname{Sp}(A) \end{array} \right\} \longleftrightarrow \left\{ \begin{array}{c} \text{rational open subsets}\\ \text{of }\operatorname{Spa}(A,A^\circ) \end{array} \right\}. \]

Finite admissible rational coverings on the rigid side correspond to ordinary finite open coverings by rational subsets on the adic side. Since rational subsets form a basis, the sheaf theory of the rigid rational \(G\)-site is thereby realized as the ordinary sheaf theory of the topological space \(\operatorname{Spa}(A,A^\circ)\).

Remark 4 (Conceptual meaning of the additional adic points). The passage from rigid spaces to adic spaces accomplishes two logically distinct tasks:

  1. Tate acyclicity proves that the prescribed rings of analytic functions satisfy the sheaf condition.

  2. The additional valuation points turn the rational Grothendieck topology into the basis of a genuine spectral topology.

The second point is essential. A family of rigid subsets may cover every classical point while still failing to be admissible. After passing to the adic spectrum, the missing valuation points make this failure visible as an ordinary failure to cover the underlying topological space.

Thus adic geometry does not discard rigid geometry. It retains its analytic functions and admissible coverings while providing a richer point set on which the former \(G\)-topology becomes an actual topology. In this precise sense, adic spaces give a more genuinely topological language for rigid geometry.

4.3 Adic spaces

The definition of an adic space is now natural: we may glue affinoid spectra precisely when their structure presheaves are sheaves.

Definition 11 (Affinoid adic space) Let \((A,A^+)\) be a complete sheafy Huber pair. The associated affinoid adic space is the triple

\[ \left( \operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+), \mathcal O_X, \{|\cdot|_x\}_{x\in X} \right), \]

consisting of the topological space \(\operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+)\), its structure sheaf of complete topological rings, and the compatible valuation carried by every point.

Definition 12 (Adic space) An adic space is a locally \(v\)-ringed space which admits an open covering by affinoid adic spaces.

Concretely, it consists of a topological space \(X\), a sheaf \(\mathcal O_X\) of complete topological rings, and a compatible valuation on every stalk \(\mathcal O_{X,x}\), such that every point has an open neighborhood isomorphic to \(\operatorname{Spa}(A,A^+)\) for some complete sheafy Huber pair \((A,A^+)\).

Morphisms of adic spaces are morphisms of locally and topologically ringed spaces that are compatible with the valuations at all points.

5 Why \(A^+\) is necessary

Let \(\operatorname{Cont}(A)\) be the space of continuous valuations on a Huber ring \(A\).

Proposition 2 (Recovering \(A^+\) from inequalities) The space \(\operatorname{Cont}(A)\) is spectral. Moreover, the following data are equivalent:

  1. A subset of the form

    \[ F= \bigcap_{f\in S} \left\{ x\in\operatorname{Cont}(A):|f(x)|\leq1 \right\} \]

    for some \(S\subseteq A^\circ\).

  2. An open integrally closed subring \(A^+\subseteq A^\circ\).

The two constructions are

\[ F\longmapsto \left\{ f\in A: |f(x)|\leq1 \text{ for every }x\in F \right\} \]

and

\[ A^+\longmapsto \left\{ x\in\operatorname{Cont}(A): |f(x)|\leq1 \text{ for every }f\in A^+ \right\}. \]

Thus \(A^+\) records precisely which inequalities have been imposed on the continuous valuations.

Example 10 (Rational localization without the positive part) Let \(A\to B\) be the rational localization associated with a rational subset \(U\subseteq\operatorname{Cont}(A)\).

In general, \(\operatorname{Cont}(B)\) is larger than \(U\). The positive subring \(B^+\) cuts out \(U\) by remembering the additional inequalities \(|b(x)|\leq1\) for \(b\in B^+\).

Consequently, the ring \(B\) alone does not remember the desired rational subset.

One also cannot impose \(A^+=A^\circ\) at every stage: this equality is not generally preserved by rational localization.
